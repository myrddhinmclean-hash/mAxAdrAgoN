# Hoard Entry: The World of Draega & the "Rivals and Destiny" Campaign

*A note on what's below: this is a working excavation of the Draega worldbuilding compendium and the 25-session arc it documents. Treat the geography as load-bearing. Treat the timeline as evidence. Treat the characters as people who, in some cases, didn't make it to the end.*

---

Draega is an archipelago. Not metaphorically — literally a sprawl of continents and islands divided by named seas, with the kind of cartographic commitment that tells you someone spent evenings naming landmasses because they liked the sound of them. Azureland, Princia, Imnia, Zhork, Frezho, the two Moxias, Solith, Verna, the Greylands, the Whitelands. That's ten major landmasses before you've drawn a coastline. A DM who builds a world this wide is either a genius or a person who needs to be stopped, and from the session logs, this one was both.

The Golden Sea and the Kladesh Ocean do most of the dramatic heavy lifting. The Golden is where ships fight. The Kladesh is where ships *disappear* — fog-shrouded, red-misted, scented with lavender and lilacs, which is the kind of atmospheric contradiction that tells you magic in Draega doesn't apologize for itself. Dragon Bay holds Braaken, and Braaken holds the Sun Dragon statue, which sits underwater and is, by every available account, *colossal*. More on that in a moment.

## The War

Two sides. That's it. The Froscius Dynasty — known to its subjects as "The Empire," a name that does zero work to disguise the fact that it's an empire — runs a brutal, expansionist regime out of Princia. Their capital, Glazhenge, is the kind of fortified you build when you know someone is coming for you. They deploy Dynasty Guards, Imperial Soldiers, the Royal Navy, and the kind of war machines that show up in paintings: mangonels, ballistae, and the Iron Fortress, a massive wooden warship that does not stay captured for long.

Against them: the Radicals. A "global uprising" is the source's phrase, and I want to sit with that for a second, because "global uprising" is also what you'd call a kitchen drawer that won't close. These are Merfolk, Dwarves, Wild Elves, and whoever else has had enough, organized around two siblings — the cleric Astrina d'Braak and her brother, the elven prince Glorinath "Glori" d'Braaken — plus a rotating cast of captains and generals. The campaign names a few: Trident, Smite, Brick, Gelda, Kooms, and the Leonin samurai Anjax Proudmane, a 7'5" vengeance-engine from Azureland whose tribe's sacred Pride Spires were destroyed by the Dynasty. Anjax is the kind of character a writer invents when they've run out of patience for diplomacy.

There are also two cults. The Order of the Silent worships Mesignio, God of Secrets, and operates out of Verna. Their initiation ritual involves clipping the wings of Aarakocra acolytes. Read that twice. The Church of the Lotus Petal, centered in Paradiso, does the opposite work: kindness, charity, sanctuary, de-escalation coaching for refugees. The Earth Cult serves Ogrémoch and deploys the Black Earth Guards, whose plate armor crumbles to dust when they die, which is either a theological statement or very inconvenient for the laundresses.

## The Cities

Braaken is the heart. A coastal metropolis ruled by King d'Braak, with a complex harbor, a sprawling underground, a grand Colosseum, and a sewer network that runs *under the front gates*. The source notes that vampires and Silent agents used those tunnels during a six-year siege of Braaken, which is the kind of detail that makes you wonder how the gates were ever considered defensive in the first place.

Glazhenge is the imperial skull. Sortengraahd is the undead capital of Imnia — dark, volcanic, overrun, dominated by a Capitol Building that, in Session 20, collapses from volcanic earthquakes mid-victory, because Draega does not let you win cleanly. FairWhether is a merchant harbor and the birthplace of the localized rebellion; Garegstown is a cliffside port under heavy Dynasty occupation with cavern systems used for smuggling. Paradiso is scenic. The source uses the word "scenic" exactly once.

## The Monuments

The Mysterious Monuments are a chain of ancient, puzzle-locked tombs, each one a trial, each one revealing a piece of a blank map leading to the Amulet of Wonder. The source lists ten. Highlights include Gladigow, built into a colossal tree, with a Craggy Room full of boulders and a perfectly smooth stone cube that I am certain some player tried to eat. The Puzzle Wall displays nineteen letters — *ZQKARLCTMDVNFWOGXPJ* — and reads "Come, human, die by sword or spear." Speaking the missing letters and the command word "ORPAR" opens it. The LIES Puzzle requires rearranging numbered tiles to spell 7135 in a seven-armed Marilith's bottom four hands; wrong answers trigger 8d6 lightning. The Seasons Room is the gentlest of the lot: a seed in fertile earth, a maiden on the walls across four engravings, and a giant beanstalk that grows through a paper-thin ceiling once you order the seasons correctly.

## The Characters, Briefly

Prince Glori is the noble fighter with the Girdle of Gearste, which thins magical fogs. He is, by every account, deeply humble, which in a D&D campaign is a red flag. Astrina is the cleric sister, assassinated in Braaken in Session 11 — the source flags this as a major turning point, and they are not exaggerating. Trident is a Level 12 Water Genasi Druid who admits to stretching the truth for a good story. Smite is a Minotaur Tempest Cleric and captain of the Iron Fortress. Brick is a Lawful Neutral Dwarven General who obeys the law even when it causes misery, which is the most D&D sentence in the entire document.

On the imperial side, Alistair Morghulis is a 23-year-old Tiefling spy with pupiless golden eyes and a permanent grin. Cawrence is an Aarakocra Cleric of the Silent whose wings were clipped as an initiation rite, raised by the Empire after being stolen as an egg. Crissaadi Severe-Storm is a 6'9" Tabaxi Monk who moves on soft pink pads and assassinates people for money.

## The Arc

Twenty-five sessions. The party meets in Zhork on the last day of Year 7999. By Session 2 they're flaming-arrowing Dynasty longboats at the FairWhether docks. By Session 5 they're escaping through cliffside caves while a massive Dynasty army watches. By Session 11 they've swum to the underwater Sun Dragon statue, placed the Dragon's Eye on its crown, and come home to find their rebel leader dead. By Session 15 the Dragon Golem is decimating the Dynasty navy and a Solar Event is causing vampires to combust in the streets. By Session 19 they've killed the Vampire King and watched his undead disintegrate. By Session 20, the Capitol is collapsing and a cosmic entity called Destiny has separated the party. By Session 23, Prince Glori is dead at the outskirts of Glazhenge, making a heroic final stand. By Session 25, a "Last Man Standing" PvP arena battle decides who crowns the new Emperor.

That is the campaign. A revolution that works, mostly, at the cost of its best people.

## The Gaps

The source itself flags contradictions. The Froscius Dynasty and "The Empire" may or may not be the same regime. The Pride Spires of Azureland and the Spires of the Morning in Waterdeep are probably not the same place, even though both appear in the source PDFs. The Vampire King's thralls disintegrating on his death contradicts the monster-sheet lore that treats vampires as independent oligarchs. And between Sessions 16 and 17, the calendar jumps a month without anyone's age tracking moving.

These are not failures. These are the seams of a homebrew world built across years of play, and the seams are where the interesting work lives.
